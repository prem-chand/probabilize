Week 13: The SVD and Complex Matrices

Learning Outcomes
  • Define the singular value decomposition A=UΣVTA = U\Sigma V^TA=UΣVT for any matrix
  • Compute singular values from the eigenvalues of ATAA^TAATA
  • Read the SVD as a rotation, a stretch along the axes and a second rotation of the unit sphere
  • Explain why singular values and eigenvalues differ, and why the SVD exists when diagonalization does not
  • Identify Hermitian and unitary matrices and their role for complex matrices
Prerequisites

Background knowledge assumed:

  • Symmetric matrices and the spectral theorem
  • Orthonormal vectors

Every matrix turns, stretches and turns#

Week 10 wrote a square matrix as A=SΛS−1A = S\Lambda S^{-1}A=SΛS−1, but only when it had a full set of independent eigenvectors. This AAA does not. A−2IA - 2IA−2I has rank 222, so the eigenvalue 222 has a single eigenvector direction, and AAA cannot be diagonalized.

The SVDSingular Value Decomposition has no such limits. Every real m×nm \times nm×n matrix factors as

A=UΣVT,A = U\Sigma V^T,A=UΣVT,

with UUU and VVV orthogonal and Σ\SigmaΣ an m×nm \times nm×n diagonal matrix of singular values σ1≥σ2≥⋯≥0\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge 0σ1​≥σ2​≥⋯≥0. Read Ax=U(Σ(VTx))Ax = U(\Sigma(V^Tx))Ax=U(Σ(VTx)) right to left, as the slider does:

  1. VTV^TVT is orthogonal, here a rotation. It moves the right singular vectors viv_ivi​ onto the axes eie_iei​ and keeps the sphere a sphere.
  2. Σ\SigmaΣ multiplies coordinate iii by σi\sigma_iσi​. The sphere becomes an ellipsoid with half-axes σi\sigma_iσi​ along the axes.
  3. UUU rotates that ellipsoid so its axes point along the left singular vectors uiu_iui​.

So Avi=σiuiAv_i = \sigma_i u_iAvi​=σi​ui​: each viv_ivi​ goes to uiu_iui​, scaled by σi\sigma_iσi​. The longest half-axis σ1\sigma_1σ1​ is the largest stretch, ∥A∥2=max⁡∥x∥=1∥Ax∥\|A\|_2 = \max_{\|x\|=1}\|Ax\|∥A∥2​=max∥x∥=1​∥Ax∥. For square AAA, the shortest half-axis σn\sigma_nσn​ is the smallest.

Constructing the SVD in the scene's numbers#

Take lengths squared: ∥Ax∥2=xT(ATA)x\|Ax\|^2 = x^T(A^TA)x∥Ax∥2=xT(ATA)x. The Gram matrix

ATA=(962684244)A^TA = \begin{pmatrix} 9 & 6 & 2 \\ 6 & 8 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & 4 \end{pmatrix}ATA=​962​684​244​​

is symmetric and positive semidefinite, so Week 12's spectral theorem applies: ATA=VΛVTA^TA = V\Lambda V^TATA=VΛVT with orthonormal viv_ivi​. Its eigenvalues are 161616, 444 and 111. Check two invariants. The trace is 9+8+4=21=16+4+19 + 8 + 4 = 21 = 16 + 4 + 19+8+4=21=16+4+1, and det⁡(ATA)=(det⁡A)2=64=16⋅4⋅1\det(A^TA) = (\det A)^2 = 64 = 16 \cdot 4 \cdot 1det(ATA)=(detA)2=64=16⋅4⋅1. Check one eigenvector:

The singular values are the square roots, σ=4,2,1\sigma = 4, 2, 1σ=4,2,1. The left singular vectors come from ui=Avi/σiu_i = Av_i/\sigma_iui​=Avi​/σi​, so u1=(4,8,8)/(3⋅4)=(1,2,2)/3u_1 = (4, 8, 8)/(3 \cdot 4) = (1, 2, 2)/3u1​=(4,8,8)/(3⋅4)=(1,2,2)/3. In general, AV=UΣAV = U\SigmaAV=UΣ column by column, and ATA=VΣTΣVTA^TA = V\Sigma^T\Sigma V^TATA=VΣTΣVT. The uiu_iui​ are also the eigenvectors of AATAA^TAAT.

Singular values are not eigenvalues. The eigenvalues of AAA are 2,2,22, 2, 22,2,2, and the singular values are 4,2,14, 2, 14,2,1. Their products agree in absolute value, since both give the volume factor: ∣det⁡A∣=2⋅2⋅2=4⋅2⋅1=8|\det A| = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 4 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 8∣detA∣=2⋅2⋅2=4⋅2⋅1=8. An eigenvector is a direction that AAA keeps. On the unit sphere, ∥Ax∥\|Ax\|∥Ax∥ is largest, smallest or stationary at the singular vectors, and their images are perpendicular. The two notions coincide for a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix, where U=V=QU = V = QU=V=Q and Σ=Λ\Sigma = \LambdaΣ=Λ, which is the Week 12 picture.

Rank. The rank is the number of positive σi\sigma_iσi​. Zeroing the smallest ones gives the best low-rank approximation (Eckart–Young), the basis of PCA in Week 14.

Complex matrices#

Over C\mathbb{C}C, each real notion has a counterpart built from the conjugate transpose AH=A‾TA^H = \overline{A}^TAH=AT:

  • Hermitian, AH=AA^H = AAH=A, is the analogue of symmetric. Its eigenvalues are real, and A=UΛUHA = U\Lambda U^HA=UΛUH.
  • Unitary, UHU=IU^HU = IUHU=I, is the analogue of orthogonal. It keeps lengths: ∥Ux∥2=xHUHUx=∥x∥2\|Ux\|^2 = x^HU^HUx = \|x\|^2∥Ux∥2=xHUHUx=∥x∥2.

Every complex m×nm \times nm×n matrix has an SVD A=UΣVHA = U\Sigma V^HA=UΣVH with unitary UUU and VVV and real σi≥0\sigma_i \ge 0σi​≥0. The discrete Fourier matrix, divided by n\sqrt{n}n​, is unitary.

Browser lab: the stretch of A between 1 and 4#

Compute the SVD of the scene's matrix and measure ∥Ax∥\|Ax\|∥Ax∥ over many unit vectors.

python · runs in browser
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

A = np.array([[2., 0., 0.],
              [2., 2., 0.],
              [1., 2., 2.]])

U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)
print("singular values =", S.round(6))
print("eigenvalues     =", np.linalg.eigvals(A).real.round(6))
print("A = U Σ Vᵀ:", np.allclose(U @ np.diag(S) @ Vt, A))
for i in range(3):
    v, u = Vt[i], U[:, i]
    print(f"v{i+1} = {np.round(3 * v).astype(int)}/3   A v = {S[i]:.0f} u:", np.allclose(A @ v, S[i] * u))

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(size=(3, 20000))
x /= np.linalg.norm(x, axis=0)
stretch = np.linalg.norm(A @ x, axis=0)
print(f"min ‖Ax‖ = {stretch.min():.3f}, max ‖Ax‖ = {stretch.max():.3f}")

plt.hist(stretch, bins=60)
plt.axvline(S[-1], color="k", linestyle="--")
plt.axvline(S[0], color="k", linestyle="--")
plt.xlabel("‖Ax‖ for unit x")
plt.ylabel("count")
plt.show()

The singular values print as [4. 2. 1.] and the eigenvalues as [2. 2. 2.]. NumPy may flip the sign of a pair ui,viu_i, v_iui​,vi​ together, so a printed viv_ivi​ can be the negative of the scene's. The product Avi=σiuiAv_i = \sigma_i u_iAvi​=σi​ui​ still holds. Every sampled stretch falls between the dashed lines at σ3=1\sigma_3 = 1σ3​=1 and σ1=4\sigma_1 = 4σ1​=4. Try A.T @ A: it is symmetric positive semidefinite, so its singular values equal its eigenvalues.

Key takeaways#

Every matrix factors as A=UΣVTA = U\Sigma V^TA=UΣVT: a rotation, a stretch along the axes, and a second rotation. The singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of ATAA^TAATA and the half-axes of the image of the unit sphere. In general they are not the eigenvalues of AAA: the scene's AAA has eigenvalues 2,2,22, 2, 22,2,2 and stretches unit vectors from 111 to 444. The SVD exists even where diagonalization fails.

Further reading#

  • Lloyd N. Trefethen & David Bau, Numerical Linear Algebra (SIAM, 1997). Lectures 4–5 derive the SVD and prove the low-rank approximation theorem (Eckart–Young).
  • Steven L. Brunton & J. Nathan Kutz, Data-Driven Science and Engineering (Cambridge University Press, 2019). Chapter 1 uses the SVD as the workhorse for data-driven low-rank structure.
