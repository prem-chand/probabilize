Composite Objectives#

Many machine learning problems have a composite structure: a smooth loss plus a nonsmooth regulariser:

min⁡x  f(x)+g(x)\min_x \; f(x) + g(x)minx​f(x)+g(x)

where fff is smooth (e.g., squared loss, logistic loss) and ggg is convex but possibly nonsmooth (e.g., ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ norm, indicator of a convex set, nuclear norm). The prototypical example is the lasso:

min⁡β  12∥Xβ−y∥22+λ∥β∥1\min_\beta \; \tfrac{1}{2}\|X\beta - y\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\beta\|_1minβ​21​∥Xβ−y∥22​+λ∥β∥1​

Gradient descent on the full objective fails because ggg is not differentiable — the subgradient of ∥β∥1\|\beta\|_1∥β∥1​ at a coordinate where βj=0\beta_j = 0βj​=0 is the entire interval [−1,1][-1, 1][−1,1], giving no unique direction.

Proximal Operator#

The proximal operator of a convex function ggg is:

prox⁡tg(v)=arg⁡min⁡x[g(x)+12t∥x−v∥22]\operatorname{prox}_{t g}(v) = \arg\min_x \left[ g(x) + \frac{1}{2t}\|x - v\|_2^2 \right]proxtg​(v)=argminx​[g(x)+2t1​∥x−v∥22​]

The prox evaluates ggg at xxx while penalising distance from vvv. Intuitively, it takes a step toward minimising ggg but stays close to the reference point vvv, with ttt controlling the trade-off. The prox is well-defined for any closed convex ggg, even nondifferentiable ones.

Key examples:

  • g(x)=∥x∥1g(x) = \|x\|_1g(x)=∥x∥1​ (lasso penalty): prox⁡t∥⋅∥1(v)=soft⁡(v,t)\operatorname{prox}_{t\|\cdot\|_1}(v) = \operatorname{soft}(v, t)proxt∥⋅∥1​​(v)=soft(v,t), where the soft-thresholding operator is soft⁡(v,t)=sign⁡(v)⋅max⁡(∣v∣−t,0)\operatorname{soft}(v, t) = \operatorname{sign}(v) \cdot \max(|v| - t, 0)soft(v,t)=sign(v)⋅max(∣v∣−t,0) applied coordinate-wise.

  • g(x)=IC(x)g(x) = I_C(x)g(x)=IC​(x) (indicator of closed convex set CCC): prox⁡IC(v)=proj⁡C(v)\operatorname{prox}_{I_C}(v) = \operatorname{proj}_C(v)proxIC​​(v)=projC​(v), the Euclidean projection onto CCC.

  • g(x)=0g(x) = 0g(x)=0: prox⁡0(v)=v\operatorname{prox}_{0}(v) = vprox0​(v)=v — prox of zero is the identity.

Worked soft-threshold example. Let v=3.7v = 3.7v=3.7, t=2.0t = 2.0t=2.0. Then soft⁡(3.7,2.0)=sign⁡(3.7)⋅(3.7−2.0)=1.7\operatorname{soft}(3.7, 2.0) = \operatorname{sign}(3.7) \cdot (3.7 - 2.0) = 1.7soft(3.7,2.0)=sign(3.7)⋅(3.7−2.0)=1.7. For v=0.5v = 0.5v=0.5 with t=2.0t = 2.0t=2.0: soft⁡(0.5,2.0)=0\operatorname{soft}(0.5, 2.0) = 0soft(0.5,2.0)=0 (shrunk to exactly zero). The threshold ttt acts as a "kill zone": any ∣v∣≤t|v| \leq t∣v∣≤t is mapped to zero, implementing sparsity.

ISTA and FISTA#

The Iterative Shrinkage-Thresholding Algorithm (ISTA) is the proximal equivalent of gradient descent for composite problems:

xk+1=prox⁡ηg(xk−η∇f(xk))x_{k+1} = \operatorname{prox}_{\eta g}\big(x_k - \eta \nabla f(x_k)\big)xk+1​=proxηg​(xk​−η∇f(xk​))

Each iteration: take a gradient step on the smooth fff, then apply the prox of the nonsmooth ggg. For lasso with f(β)=12∥Xβ−y∥22f(\beta) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|X\beta - y\|_2^2f(β)=21​∥Xβ−y∥22​ and g(β)=λ∥β∥1g(\beta) = \lambda\|\beta\|_1g(β)=λ∥β∥1​:

βk+1=soft⁡(βk−ηXT(Xβk−y),  ηλ)\beta_{k+1} = \operatorname{soft}\big(\beta_k - \eta X^T(X\beta_k - y),\; \eta\lambda\big)βk+1​=soft(βk​−ηXT(Xβk​−y),ηλ)

ISTA converges at rate O(1/k)O(1/k)O(1/k) — the same as gradient descent on smooth problems. This is remarkable: the nondifferentiable ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ term does not degrade the worst-case rate.

FISTA (Fast ISTA) adds Nesterov momentum to achieve O(1/k2)O(1/k^2)O(1/k2) — the optimal rate for first-order methods on this class:

yk+1=xk+tk−1tk+1(xk−xk−1),xk+1=prox⁡ηg(yk+1−η∇f(yk+1))y_{k+1} = x_k + \frac{t_k - 1}{t_{k+1}}(x_k - x_{k-1}), \quad x_{k+1} = \operatorname{prox}_{\eta g}(y_{k+1} - \eta \nabla f(y_{k+1}))yk+1​=xk​+tk+1​tk​−1​(xk​−xk−1​),xk+1​=proxηg​(yk+1​−η∇f(yk+1​))

where tk+1=(1+1+4tk2)/2t_{k+1} = (1 + \sqrt{1 + 4 t_k^2})/2tk+1​=(1+1+4tk2​​)/2 is the Nesterov sequence. The acceleration comes from extrapolation — evaluating the gradient at a point beyond the current iterate, extrapolated in the direction of recent progress.

Why slow convergence is acceptable for lasso. Lasso is typically solved once per λ\lambdaλ for a fixed dataset of moderate dimension. O(1/k2)O(1/k^2)O(1/k2) with a well-tuned step size converges to high accuracy in hundreds of iterations — seconds in practice. The simplicity of ISTA/FISTA (no linear system solve) makes them the go-to first-order choice for structured convex problems.

Operator Splitting and ADMM#

ADMMAlternating Direction Method of Multipliers (Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers) solves problems of the form:

min⁡x,z  f(x)+g(z)s.t.Ax+Bz=c\min_{x, z} \; f(x) + g(z) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad Ax + Bz = cminx,z​f(x)+g(z)s.t.Ax+Bz=c

where fff and ggg are convex (possibly nonsmooth). The variables xxx and zzz are decoupled in the objective but coupled through a linear constraint. ADMM iterates:

x_{k+1} &= \arg\min_x \left[ f(x) + \frac{\rho}{2}\|Ax + Bz_k - c + u_k\|_2^2 \right] \quad (\text{$x$-update})\\[4pt] z_{k+1} &= \arg\min_z \left[ g(z) + \frac{\rho}{2}\|Ax_{k+1} + Bz - c + u_k\|_2^2 \right] \quad (\text{$z$-update})\\[4pt] u_{k+1} &= u_k + Ax_{k+1} + Bz_{k+1} - c \quad (\text{dual update}) \end{aligned}$$ where $\rho > 0$ is a penalty parameter and $u$ is the scaled dual variable. Each $x$- and $z$-subproblem involves only one of the two objective terms — this is the **splitting** that gives ADMM its power. When $f$ and $g$ have simple proximal operators (e.g., $f$ is a quadratic, $g$ is $\ell_1$), each subproblem is cheap. **Consensus ADMM.** A common pattern is $f(x) = \sum_i f_i(x_i)$ and $g$ enforces consensus $x_i = z$ for all $i$. Each $f_i$ can be handled independently (in parallel), then the $z$-update averages the results. This maps directly to distributed optimisation. **When ADMM helps:** - Problems that decompose naturally into two blocks with cheap prox operators (e.g., lasso in consensus form across data shards). - Distributed settings where the $x$-update can be parallelised. - Problems where an IPM is too expensive but ISTA on the monolithic problem converges slowly due to coupling. **When ADMM is slower:** For tightly coupled objectives where the $x$- and $z$-updates are as hard as the original problem, ADMM adds overhead (the penalty parameter $\rho$ and dual variable $u$) without reducing per-iteration cost. ## Choosing Prox vs IPM vs SGD A quick decision guide for structured problems: | Situation | Method | |---|---| | Small-medium LP/QP/SOCP, need high accuracy | IPM | | Composite $f + g$ with simple $\operatorname{prox}_g$ | ISTA / FISTA | | Multi-block, distributed, or consensus structure | ADMM | | Nonconvex, huge data, general structure | <Glossary term="SGD" /> / <Glossary term="Adam" /> | | $\ell_1$ / nuclear norm regularisation with known $\operatorname{prox}$ | ISTA / FISTA | The patterns repeat across applications: find the structure, match it to the method, and exploit cheap proximal operators when they exist. <Exercise type="fill-blank" question="Soft-threshold $\operatorname{soft}(v, t)$ applied to $v = 0.8$ with $t = 1.5$ produces ____." correctAnswer="0" explanation="$\operatorname{soft}(0.8, 1.5) = \operatorname{sign}(0.8) \cdot \max(0.8 - 1.5, 0) = \max(-0.7, 0) = 0$.
